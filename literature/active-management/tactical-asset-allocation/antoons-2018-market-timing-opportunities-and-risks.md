## What it is

An asset manager's case against market timing, built mostly from S&P 500 history and a
survey of older studies. It concludes that strategic allocation, with at most small
tactical tilts (the example is 50/50 allowed to move to 45/55), is the sound approach.

- **What perfect foresight would be worth.** Putting everything each year into the coming
  year's best asset class (large caps, T-bills or long Treasuries), 1925–2015, turns $1
  into about $3.7 million. That is the prize timers chase.
- **Bull and bear markets, 1927–2015.** 12 bear markets (falls of more than 20%) and 13
  bull markets. Average bull gain 179.8% over 66 months; average bear loss −35.75% over 16
  months; 27% of months inside bear markets were positive. The first three months after a
  20%+ fall gained 21.4% on average, the period timers sitting in cash tend to miss.
- **Bulls matter more than bears.** Citing Chua and Woodward (1986): forecasting only half
  of bull markets underperformed buy-and-hold even with perfect bear forecasts. Timing paid
  only with accuracy of at least 80% of bulls and 50% of bears, 70% and 80%, or 60% and 90%.
- **Best and worst days, 1961–2015.** Buy-and-hold returned 9.87% a year. Missing the 25
  worst days gives 15.27%; missing the 25 best gives 5.74%; missing both gives 10.94%. The
  best 81 days (0.59% of 13,844) produced the whole buy-and-hold return; without them the
  return falls to 0.03% a year. The best and worst days cluster together, nearly half of
  the 50 around 2008, so avoiding the worst days in practice means missing the best ones.
- **The record of actual timers.** Graham and Harvey's 237 newsletters (1980–1992) were
  right under 25% of the time. CXO Advisory's 68 gurus (6,582 forecasts, 1998–2012): 42
  were right under half the time and the best 68.2%. Dalbar: over 30 years to 2015 the
  S&P 500 returned 10.35% a year and the average equity fund investor 3.66%. A South
  African study needed 87.4% switch accuracy to beat buy-and-hold and lost more from wrong
  timing than it gained from right timing.
- **Why timers persist.** Overconfidence and the difficulty of reversing a wrong call
  (anchoring and loss aversion), with quotations from Keynes and Swensen.

## Why it's in the library

It is the sceptic's side of the tactical-allocation and bear-market material: the cost of
being out of the market when a bear market ends. The 21.4% average gain in the first three
months after a 20% fall, and the clustering of best and worst days, are the numbers any
bear-market exit rule has to beat.

Read it as advocacy built on summary statistics, not as a test. The best/worst-days
figures assume perfect hindsight in both directions, which no rule has, and say nothing
about rules that cut exposure gradually (trend or volatility filters) rather than going all
in or all out. The Dalbar investor-return gap is a disputed measure, and several cited
studies are decades old. It is also inconsistent in places: the best/worst-days test is
described as using monthly returns but reports daily results. The piece does not test any
systematic timing rule itself.
