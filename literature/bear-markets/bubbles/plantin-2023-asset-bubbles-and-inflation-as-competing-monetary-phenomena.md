## What it is

A model of the popular claim that easy monetary policy "creates bubbles instead of
investment, and the bubbles burst when inflation comes back". The paper builds the simplest
economy in which that story holds as an equilibrium outcome.

- **Ingredients.** Overlapping generations (so rational bubbles can exist), entrepreneurs who
  can pledge only part of their project returns to lenders (a financial friction), firms
  that pay a fixed menu cost to change prices, and a central bank that follows a Taylor rule.
- **Two kinds of equilibrium.** Because changing prices pays more when others do too, the
  menu cost creates both a flexible-price equilibrium and a rigid-price one. With rigid
  prices and a positive inflation target, the Taylor rule leaves the policy rate below the
  natural rate.
- **Natural vs monetary bubbles.** A *natural* bubble can exist only if the natural rate is
  low; it pushes interest rates up and may help investment by easing financial constraints.
  A *monetary* bubble arises in the rigid-price equilibrium even when the natural rate is
  high. It differs in three ways:
  1. it does not raise the interest rate however large it gets, so it earns a low return
     itself;
  2. it cannot coexist with high CPI inflation, so it bursts when inflation picks up;
  3. it always crowds out investment, by moving resources from constrained entrepreneurs to
     savers who buy the bubble.
- **A sunspot path.** Proposition 7 builds an equilibrium with no fundamental shock: prices
  are rigid, rates are low and a bubble grows; at a random date firms coordinate on
  repricing, inflation jumps to target, rates rise and the bubble bursts.

## Why it's in the library

**It turns a market narrative into a model with testable features.** The predictions are
specific: bubbles that coincide with low rates, low inflation and weak investment, that earn
low returns, and that end when inflation and policy rates rise. That is a clean frame for
reading the 2020–2022 cycle, when inflation returned and speculative assets fell together.

**It is theory only.** The paper calibrates nothing and tests nothing. The multiplicity of
equilibria comes from one modelling choice (a fixed menu cost with strategic
complementarity in price setting), and the author notes that enough heterogeneity across
firms would remove it.
