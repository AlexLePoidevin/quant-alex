## What it is

A study of who made and who lost money in a real bubble, using shareholder registers that
name every investor, their occupation and their address.

- **The episode.** British bicycle company shares nearly trebled in early 1896, peaked
  again in March 1897, and then lost 73% by the end of 1898 (index 241 in March 1897, 66 in
  January 1899). Between January 1896 and June 1897, 601 new cycle companies were set up.
  All 25 sample companies later went bankrupt or were wound up at heavy losses, so anyone
  holding after the peak lost money.
- **The data.** By law, companies had to publish each shareholder's name, occupation,
  address and holding every year. The authors use registers from before and after the
  peak for 25 cycle, tube and tyre companies (12,167 shareholders with an occupation), plus
  1,996 recorded share sales from 10 of them. The published version adds a control group
  of 11 non-cycle companies founded in 1895–98 that did not crash (10,586 shareholders).
- **Who bought in.** Compared with the control firms, cycle investors were more often
  merchants, manufacturers, engineers and people in finance, and less often gentlemen
  (men of independent means) or women. Groups thought of as naïve, such as clergy, farmers
  and labourers, were barely involved.
- **Who got out.** Company directors held 25.6% of their own firms' shares before the peak
  and 20.1% after it, while directors of the control firms increased their stakes.
  Cycle-industry workers and people living near the company office also reduced their
  holdings. That is consistent with insiders using information to sell, often at a
  profit.
- **Who was left holding.** Gentlemen went from 15.6% of cycle shareholders to 31.0%. The
  clearest losers were gentlemen living near a stock exchange (London or Birmingham):
  1.86% of shareholders before the peak, but 19.06% of all recorded sales and 8.93% of
  shareholders after it. So they traded heavily, both selling and buying, and ended up
  holding. In a logit regression, living near an exchange only raised the chance of
  holding after the crash for gentlemen; for everyone else it made no difference.
- **The explanation offered.** Wealthy men with free time and an exchange nearby were not
  the least informed investors. The authors read their losses as overconfidence and
  familiarity bias: enough information to think they had an edge, and the time and
  opportunity to trade on it.

## Why it's in the library

**It tests the usual story about bubbles and partly overturns it.** The popular account
is that the last buyers are the least sophisticated: shoeshine boys and taxi drivers.
Here the people left holding were wealthy, active, exchange-adjacent traders, the profile
of today's frequent speculative trader. Insiders, meanwhile, sold.

**It is rare data.** Most studies of bubble investors see either fund types (hedge funds,
mutual funds) or a single company. This one sees every named shareholder across many
companies, before and after the peak, with a control group.

**Limits.** It is one mania in one country in the 1890s. Occupation is a rough proxy for
information and experience, and buyers are not recorded, so who bought is inferred from
sales plus the change in holdings. The control firms are larger and the gap between
their registers is longer (54 months against 22).

**The two versions differ.** The 2021 working paper also tested Abreu and Brunnermeier's
"riding the bubble" model directly, finding that recorded sales spiked in March 1897, the
month of the peak, consistent with informed sellers coordinating their exit. The
published version drops that test, adds the control group and the difference-in-
differences analysis, and recasts the result around familiarity and overconfidence.
