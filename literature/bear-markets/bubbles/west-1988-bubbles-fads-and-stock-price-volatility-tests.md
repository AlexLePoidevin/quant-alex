## What it is

A participant's summary of the excess-volatility literature that followed Shiller (1981)
and LeRoy and Porter (1981), and of whether bubbles, small samples, time-varying expected
returns or "fads" explain it.

- **The tests.** Each study compares the volatility of the market's forecast of discounted
  dividends with the volatility of a measure of fundamentals, summarised as a ratio V/V*
  (above 1 means excess volatility). Early tests that assumed stationarity found violations
  of orders of magnitude.
- **Small-sample bias.** Once unit roots in dividends are allowed for, estimated excess
  volatility falls by about an order of magnitude but does not disappear. West concludes
  that bias is unlikely to explain all of it.
- **Rational bubbles don't fit well.** A negative bubble is impossible (it would force the
  price below zero). With infinitely lived agents positive bubbles are ruled out too;
  overlapping-generations models allow them only if the economy grows faster than the
  return on the asset. Real US stock returns averaged about 8% a year against GNP growth of
  about 3%, so that condition looks hard to meet. A bubble must also have existed from the
  first day of trading.
- **Standard time-varying-return models fail.** Consumption-based and similar models do not
  generate enough variation in expected returns.
- **Fads.** Models in which naive noise traders create risk that sophisticated investors
  must bear could explain the volatility, but in 1988 there was little direct evidence
  for them.

## Why it's in the library

**It is the clearest short statement of why rational bubbles are a poor explanation of
stock price swings.** The constraints it lists (no negative bubbles, no bubble starting
after trading begins, growth above the interest rate) still frame the bubble literature.

**It is dated.** It predates habit-formation models and the large literature on predictable
returns from valuation ratios, which now carry most of what West called "nonstandard
models for expected returns".
