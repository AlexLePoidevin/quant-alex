## What it is

A model in which the 2008 crisis is the bursting of a bubble, a shift in investor
sentiment rather than a shock to productivity, and in which the bubble had been doing
something useful before it burst.

- **The setup.** A standard "financial accelerator" economy (overlapping generations, in
  the Gertler–Kiyotaki spirit). New firms invest more efficiently than old ones, but
  they can borrow only against a fraction of their future resources, including their
  future market value. Their net worth is their collateral.
- **Bubbles as pyramid schemes.** Firm prices can exceed the replacement cost of their
  capital in equilibrium. A bubble must grow at the interest rate to be worth holding,
  but not so fast that the young cannot afford to buy it. Which equilibrium the economy
  is in is set by investor sentiment, a sunspot. Creating and selling a bubble is "a
  transfer from the future": entrepreneurs effectively sell claims on the savings of
  later generations.
- **Expansionary bubbles.** With financial frictions, a bubble raises the market value of
  new firms and so their borrowing capacity. That moves investment towards the efficient
  firms, which can outweigh the savings diverted into buying the bubble. Such a bubble
  raises the capital stock and output and lowers the interest rate. This is unlike the
  classic Tirole bubble, which crowds out investment.
- **The crisis read this way.** A long boom (the bubbly episode from the early 1990s to
  2007–08) with rising asset prices and credit, then a sudden collapse when sentiment
  flips. Net worth falls, credit contracts and output drops, even though technology and
  resources are unchanged.
- **Fiscal policy can replace the bubble, if it is credible.** Government debt is also a
  claim on future generations. If debt is issued to match the value of the lost bubble,
  and the proceeds subsidise productive new firms, the economy can stay on its pre-crisis
  path, and the scheme even makes a profit because government debt carries less risk
  than the bubble. But if investors may refuse to roll the debt over, the policy loses
  money and can become impossible: the crisis then moves from private markets to
  sovereign debt markets.
- **International spread.** If one country's bubble bursts, wages and net worth fall
  everywhere, and bubble creation shifts across countries, so a local shift in sentiment
  becomes a global recession. Spending should rise in productive countries, and debt
  should be issued by the most credible governments, which sets up conflict between
  countries.

## Why it's in the library

**It shows how a bubble can matter for the real economy without anything real changing.**
In most finance models a bubble is a mispricing that redistributes wealth between
traders. Here it is collateral: it lets efficient firms borrow, so its collapse cuts
investment and output. That is a coherent story for why some crashes, such as 2008, are
followed by deep recessions while others are not.

**It connects bubbles to sovereign debt.** The model's reading of 2010–12 is that
governments tried to replace a private bubble with public debt, and the euro-area debt
crisis was the market doubting that replacement.

**It is theory, written during the crisis.** The authors call these "notes", the
calibrated figures are illustrations, and nothing is estimated. The model has no
unemployment, no money and no explicit government objectives, all of which the authors
list as open extensions. The bubble is a sunspot, so the model says what a bubble does,
not why one starts or when it ends.
