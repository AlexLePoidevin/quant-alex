## What it is

A method for spotting price bubbles without modelling fundamental value, by training a
neural network to recognise when a price process behaves like a bubble in the mathematical
sense.

- **The theory it relies on.** In the Jarrow–Protter framework a bubble is a price above
  the expected discounted value of future cash flows. In continuous time with a finite
  horizon, an asset is in a bubble exactly when its discounted price is a *strict local
  martingale* under the risk-neutral measure: a process with no drift that nonetheless
  tends to fall, because rare enormous upside paths are missing. Whether that holds depends
  only on how volatility grows with the price level. With volatility proportional to
  price to the power γ₁, the price is a bubble when γ₁ > 1 and a true martingale when
  1/2 < γ₁ ≤ 1. No model of fundamentals is needed, which avoids the usual joint-hypothesis
  problem.
- **The existing estimator and its weakness.** Obayashi, Protter and Yang (2017) fit the
  volatility function over rolling 21-day windows, then smooth the bubble signal with a
  hidden Markov model. Windows that straddle a regime change mix the two regimes and give
  wrong parameters.
- **The network.** Two bidirectional LSTM layers (100 units each) classify each point of a
  price series as "true martingale" or "strict local martingale". It is trained on
  simulated prices whose volatility switches between bubble and non-bubble regimes by a
  Markov chain, so the labels are known exactly.
- **Simulation test.** On 150 out-of-sample simulated paths (2-minute data, three years,
  parameters never seen in training) the network classifies 83.6% of points correctly
  against 49.7% for the rolling-window estimator, and runs in under an hour against about
  43 hours.
- **Real data.** Thirty Dow Jones stocks at 2-minute frequency, 2006–2008, with jumps
  removed first using the authors' earlier method. A zero-net-exposure strategy shorts each
  stock while it is classified as a bubble and holds the Dow index long against it. The
  paper shows the portfolio value only as a chart (Figure 6.1) and gives no return,
  volatility or cost figures.
- **The discrete-time objection.** Strict local martingales exist only in continuous time.
  The authors argue that if prices really were discrete, the network would just be
  detecting periods of skewed returns (frequent small falls, rare large rises), and the
  long-short strategy should then not make money. They read its profitability as support
  for the continuous-time view.

## Why it's in the library

**It is a clean statement of what "bubble" can mean in a testable way.** The
strict-local-martingale definition turns a vague idea into a property of the volatility
function, which can be estimated from prices alone. That is a useful reference point next to
behavioural accounts of bubbles and crashes.

**The simulation evidence is real but narrow.** The network beats the existing estimator on
data generated by the same family of models it was trained on (power-law volatility with
Markov switching). That shows it handles regime changes better; it does not show how it
behaves when real volatility follows some other shape.

**The trading evidence is weak, and the paper does not address the obvious problems.**
- The bidirectional LSTM classifies each point using data from *after* it as well as
  before. The paper describes this as letting the network "use the whole dataset", and does
  not say the strategy was run on rolling, past-only windows. If it was not, the backtest
  has look-ahead.
- The sample is 2006–2008, a period when shorting individual stocks against the index was
  helped by the crash itself.
- There are no costs, no statistics and no numbers, only a chart.

Treat the empirical part as an illustration, not evidence that the bubbles it flags are
tradable.
