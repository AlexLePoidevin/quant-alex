## What it is

Geithner, president of the New York Fed during the 2007–09 crisis, answers Metrick's
questions on why the Fed rescued Bear Stearns and AIG but not Lehman Brothers. The answers
are a first-hand account and a defence of those decisions.

- **Why nonbanks mattered.** By early 2008 commercial banks supplied under 40% of the credit
  the financial system gave US households and businesses, down from nearly 70% in 1980. The
  rest came from investment banks, money funds, Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, AIG and others that
  had thin capital, heavy short-term funding and no deposit insurance or regular access to
  the Fed.
- **What the Fed could and could not do.** Under Section 13(3) it could lend to a nonbank only
  in "unusual and exigent" conditions, when no private funding was available, and only
  against collateral judged to cover the loan. It could not inject capital or guarantee
  liabilities; only Congress could authorise that, which it did with TARP in October 2008.
- **Bear Stearns, March 2008.** Lending into the run would only have financed the exit of the
  quickest creditors. The Fed instead arranged JP Morgan's purchase, lending to a vehicle
  holding about $30 billion of Bear's assets, with JP Morgan taking the first $1 billion of
  losses. The New York Fed eventually earned almost $700 million on the portfolio.
- **Lehman, September 2008.** Geithner says the problem was the same as Bear's, and that the
  outcome differed only because there was no buyer. Lehman was over one and a half times
  Bear's size, its likely losses were larger, Bank of America walked away, and UK regulators
  would not let Barclays guarantee Lehman's obligations before a shareholder vote. A loan
  against Lehman's collateral would not have stopped the run. The Fed did lend to Lehman's
  broker-dealer (peaking at $68 billion) until Barclays bought it.
- **AIG, days later.** The Fed judged that a fully collateralised $85 billion loan could keep
  AIG operating, because it had valuable regulated insurance subsidiaries. The loans were
  repaid with about $20 billion of profit.
- **Lessons.** Don't let the financial system outgrow its protections; give the authorities
  the full set of crisis tools in peacetime; in an extreme crisis, do whatever it takes to
  protect the core of the system. Geithner judges the post-crisis reforms (more capital, new
  resolution powers) to have made the system much stronger, but argues that Congress also
  removed emergency powers that a future crisis would need.

## Why it's in the library

It explains the mechanics behind the worst part of the 2008 bear market: a run on
institutions funded short-term, a lender of last resort that could lend against collateral
but not supply capital, and a panic that intensified when markets saw the authorities
running out of tools. Lehman, in Ed Lazear's metaphor that Geithner quotes, was one of the
first kernels to pop in a pan that was already too hot.

It is one side's account, written by a decision-maker defending his choices; the claim that
the law left no option on Lehman has been disputed by others, and the paper does not engage
with those critiques in detail.
