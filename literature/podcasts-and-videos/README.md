The split is the point. A recorded lecture is a primary source on someone's argument; a
documentary is someone else's account, built backwards from an outcome the maker already
knows. They fail in different ways and have to be read differently.

## How to read anything in this folder

Four things are true of recorded material that are not true of a paper, and they apply
before you get to the specific source:

**No referee and no discussant.** Nobody had to be satisfied for this to be published. The
speaker sets the frame, chooses the examples, and is never made to answer the strongest
objection.

**Interviews are performances.** The host steers, the audience is general, and the framework
gets simplified to fit the segment. Self-reported accounts of one's own trades are the
weakest evidence in finance — being right about a famous trade is not evidence that the
stated theory is why.

**Not citable at the resolution a paper is.** You can cite a page; a timestamp is fragile,
the video can be taken down, and nobody can check your reading against a fixed text.

**Best use is calibration, not authority.** Recorded sources are good for how a person
frames an idea, what they emphasise when unconstrained, and how that changes over time —
which is genuinely useful, and is not the same as evidence. Where a refereed statement of
the same argument exists, cite that instead.
