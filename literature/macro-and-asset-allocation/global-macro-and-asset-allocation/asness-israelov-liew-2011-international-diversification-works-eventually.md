## What it is

A reply to the critique that international diversification fails when it is needed,
because markets crash together. The authors agree with the observation and argue it is
the wrong horizon.

For investors in each of 22 countries they compare a home-market portfolio with an
equal-weighted, unhedged global portfolio, in real local-currency terms:

- **Short horizons: diversification disappoints.** In each country's worst month the
  local market fell 27.0% on average and the global portfolio still fell 17.2%. The worst
  months of the global portfolios averaged −23.3% (a −5.4 standard deviation event)
  against −27.0% (−4.6 sd) for the local ones. Because markets crash together while
  diversification cuts normal volatility, the global portfolio is more negatively skewed
  (about −1.0 to −1.2 against −0.6 to −0.7 monthly). For a US investor, the worst local
  month was October 1987 (−21.4%); the worst global month was October 2008 (−24.2%).
- **Long horizons: it works.** Over 60 months the average worst local real return was
  −57%; in those same periods the global portfolio lost 16% on average, and the worst
  five-year global return averaged −39%. The skewness gap closes by about 3.5–5 years.
  Cap-weighted global portfolios (about half US, 18% Japan) show much smaller long-run
  gains, which the authors attribute to concentration and an anti-value tilt.
- **Why.** Returns are split into valuation-multiple changes and economic performance
  (dividend growth plus dividends), each into global and country-specific parts. Over a
  quarter, multiple changes explain 96% of return variance, and the global multiple alone
  51%: panics are global. Over 15 years, country-specific economic performance explains
  39% and rising. Japan's lost decade was a domestic economic failure, not a global panic,
  and that is the risk diversification protects against.

## Why it's in the library

It separates two kinds of bad outcome that are often confused: a short, global crash
driven by risk aversion, against which country diversification does little, and a long,
country-specific bear market driven by fundamentals, against which it does a great deal.
The finding that diversification makes the portfolio's short-run tail worse (more
negative skew) while improving its long-run tail is a useful caution for anyone sizing
leverage on diversified volatility.

The global portfolio is equal-weighted, which the authors concede most large
institutions cannot hold, and the sample ends in December 2008, before a long period of
US outperformance that tested the argument. The worst-case statistics are averages
across 22 overlapping country histories, so they are not independent observations.
