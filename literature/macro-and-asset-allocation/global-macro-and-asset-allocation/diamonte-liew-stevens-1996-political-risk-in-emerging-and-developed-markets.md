## What it is

A short empirical paper asking whether changes in political risk move country stock
returns, using the International Country Risk Guide (ICRG) monthly political-risk score
(13 components such as leadership, corruption, external conflict and military in politics,
scaled 0–100) for 21 developed and 24 emerging markets, January 1985 to June 1995.

- **Method.** Each quarter, countries are split into those whose political risk fell
  ("upgrades") and rose ("downgrades"), and each portfolio weights countries by the size
  of their *same-quarter* risk change.
- **Emerging markets.** Upgrades returned 12.4% a quarter and downgrades 1.1%, a gap of
  11.3% a quarter (t = 3.90).
- **Developed markets.** The gap was 2.5% a quarter (t = 1.84), not significant. The
  emerging-minus-developed difference in the gap was 8.8% a quarter (t = 2.72).
- **Sensitivity.** Per unit of risk change, emerging returns moved 2.81% against 0.74% for
  developed markets, so it is not only that emerging political risk moves more.
- **Convergence.** Over the decade, political risk fell in 21 of 24 emerging markets and
  rose in 19 of 21 developed ones.

## Why it's in the library

It is an early, simple piece of evidence that country returns in emerging markets carry a
priced, measurable political component, and an early paper by people (Liew, later an AQR
founder) whose macro work fills this section. The convergence finding is a reminder that a
"developed versus emerging" risk label is not fixed.

The returns are contemporaneous: the portfolios are formed on risk changes in the same
quarter as the returns, so the paper shows association, not a tradable forecast. The
authors say as much ("if one can forecast changes in political risk, one can forecast stock
returns"). Ten years of quarterly data and analyst ratings that may react to prices are
further limits.
