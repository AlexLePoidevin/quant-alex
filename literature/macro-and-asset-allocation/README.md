# Macro and asset allocation

The macro side of investing: gold and the dollar, inflation and monetary policy, why the stock–bond correlation changes sign, which assets are safe havens when markets fall, and global macro and multi-asset allocation.

## Gold and the dollar

- [[baur-2021-is-gold-a-safe-haven-in-all-currencies]]: Gold's safe-haven property in 68 stock markets depends on the investor's currency: priced in a currency that falls in crises gold is a strong haven, while for dollar, yen and franc investors it adds little in the acute phase.
- [[baur-et-al-2020-investing-in-gold-market-timing-or-buy-and-hold]]: More than 4,000 seasonal, technical and fundamental rules for timing gold, 1990–2017: the best beat buy-and-hold by 2–3% a year in-sample, but none survives Hansen's SPA test for data snooping.
- [[baur-kuck-2020-the-timing-of-the-flight-to-gold]]: Five-minute gold and S&P 500 data, 2007–2018: on the worst crash days gold rises about 1% on average, only once the equity fall passes about 3% and keeps building after the US close, and on single 5-minute plunges it is merely uncorrelated (a weak safe haven).
- [[baur-smales-2018-gold-and-geopolitical-risk]]: Gold, alone among precious metals, copper, stocks and Bitcoin, rises with the newspaper-based geopolitical risk index (1985–2017), driven by threats rather than realised events and distinct from VIX-measured financial fear.
- [[baur-tran-2014-the-long-run-relationship-of-gold-and-silver]]: Gold and silver prices, 1970–2011, look co-integrated only because of extreme episodes (the 1979–80 silver corner, the 2008 crisis, 2010–11); in normal periods the link disappears, and gold leads silver.
- [[baur-trench-2022-not-all-gold-shines-in-crisis-times]]: 230 ASX gold explorers, developers and producers in the COVID crash: in normal times their gold and market betas follow their business model, but in the crash all fell with the market while gold rose — gold shares are not a safe haven.
- [[capie-mills-wood-2005-gold-as-a-hedge-against-the-dollar]]: Weekly gold against sterling–dollar and yen–dollar, 1971–2004: gold is an inelastic dollar hedge (elasticity about −0.2 to −0.3) whose strength shifts sharply between sub-periods.
- [[christensen-madsen-2022-the-us-dollar-and-fed-tightening-cycles]]: A bank note walking EUR/USD through the Fed's four hiking cycles from 1994 to 2018: the dollar did not reliably strengthen, because other themes (the euro's launch, the US trade deficit, ECB policy) dominated each time.
- [[daly-2005-tactical-asset-allocation-to-gold]]: An MBA working paper that holds gold whenever the Fed model shows stocks 15%+ undervalued, with large in-sample returns; useful mainly as an example of a hand-picked threshold, look-ahead earnings and no significance test.
- [[erb-harvey-2013-the-golden-dilemma]]: Erb and Harvey test the six usual reasons to own gold (inflation hedge, currency hedge, low real yields, safe haven, gold as money, underowned) and find most fail at investment horizons; the real gold price works as a noisy valuation ratio.
- [[erb-harvey-2016-the-golden-constant]]: A golden-constant fair value for gold ($840 an ounce in 2016, with a possible low of $353) and ten-year real return scenarios of −4.4% to −12.4% a year; a long-horizon mean-reversion forecast that the following decade did not bear out.
- [[erb-harvey-viskanta-2020-gold-the-golden-constant-and-massive-passives]]: Erb, Harvey and Viskanta in mid-2020: gold's return is driven by its real price rather than inflation, the real price was near its 1980 and 2011 peaks, gold-versus-real-yields fails a cointegration test, and ETF 'massive passive' holdings may push prices higher anyway.
- [[feldstein-1978-the-effect-of-inflation-on-the-prices-of-land-and-gold]]: Feldstein's tax explanation for why land and gold outran consumer prices in the 1970s: with unindexed taxes, higher expected inflation hurts bonds' after-tax real yield more than gold's or land's, so their relative prices jump.
- [[jermann-2022-golds-value-as-an-investment]]: A no-arbitrage model in which investors hold gold as a yieldless store of value with an embedded put (users buy at a floor), explaining why gold tracks real yields mainly when rates are low and fitting gold prices since 2007.
- [[peterson-2018-when-to-own-stocks-and-when-to-own-gold]]: A secular-cycle switch between US stocks and gold using Shiller's CAPE divided by the log gold price: since 1886 it beats both buy-and-hold portfolios in about 70% of ten-year windows, but its thresholds were picked by eye over the whole sample and it trades only a handful of times.
- [[stavrakeva-tang-2019-the-dollar-during-the-great-recession]]: Why US monetary easing strengthened the dollar in 2008–2012: calendar-based forward guidance signalled a weak economy, raising risk aversion (a flight to safety) and lowering expected US inflation, with the dollar rising most against currencies that are poor hedges for US growth.

## Inflation and monetary policy

- [[ang-bekaert-2002-regime-switches-in-interest-rates]]: Regime-switching models of US, German and UK short rates (1972–1996): a calm near-random-walk regime and a volatile mean-reverting one that lines up with US recessions; regime models forecast better out of sample but match sample moments worse than one-regime models.
- [[aqr-2017-exploring-rates-sensitivity]]: AQR on how asset classes, equity styles, trend, 60/40 and risk parity behaved during Fed hiking cycles and moves in real yields and expected inflation, 1972–2016, plus a +1.8% rising-yields scenario.
- [[aqr-2020-fire-and-ice-inflation-and-deflation]]: AQR's growth-controlled inflation sensitivity of stocks, bonds, credit, TIPS, breakevens, commodities, gold, risk parity, trend and macro momentum since 1972, and why nominal bonds hedge deflation but not inflation.
- [[aqr-2021-inflation-outlook-the-macro-the-micro-the-transitory]]: AQR's October 2021 decomposition of the US CPI surge into reopening, autos, energy and an accelerating residual, arguing against a purely transitory reading (a dated view that proved right).
- [[barthelemy-mengus-plantin-2021-the-central-bank-the-treasury-or-the-market]]: A game between treasury, central bank and bond investors: a central bank without commitment can still hold its price-level target if large deficits push real rates up enough to make inflating away debt not worth it; otherwise fiscal dominance sets the price level, possibly only after years of low rates.
- [[bekaert-hoerova-lo-duca-2013-risk-uncertainty-and-monetary-policy]]: Splits the VIX into expected volatility ('uncertainty') and the variance premium ('risk aversion') and finds in VARs that looser US monetary policy lowers risk aversion from about nine months on for over two years; the effect is clear pre-2007 and weaker with the crisis included.
- [[bjornland-et-al-2021-oil-and-fiscal-policy-regimes]]: A Bayesian Markov-switching panel of 23 oil exporters: governments switch repeatedly between procyclical and countercyclical spending, and non-OECD and OPEC states spend oil windfalls more aggressively than OECD ones.
- [[brooks-2021-what-drives-bond-yields]]: AQR's framework for government bond yields: short rate, expected path and term premium; central banks steer the near end, while long yields move about one-for-one with long-run growth and inflation expectations.
- [[cieslak-vissing-jorgensen-2021-the-economics-of-the-fed-put]]: Since the mid-1990s a 10% fall in stocks between FOMC meetings predicts a 32bp lower fed funds target at the next meeting and 127bp after a year, mainly through downgrades to the Fed's growth forecasts; FOMC text shows the Fed watches the market chiefly for its wealth effect on consumption.
- [[colavecchio-rubene-2020-non-linear-exchange-rate-pass-through-to-euro-area-inflation]]: How much of a euro move reaches euro area prices, 1997–2019: about 20% of import prices on impact and 33% after a year, only a few percent of consumer prices, with large moves passing through more than small ones and no difference between appreciations and depreciations.
- [[conrad-2021-money-supply-interest-rates-and-stock-prices-two-experiments]]: Two small classroom experiments in which more money and falling rates pushed student bids for shares up, and stopping the expansion or hiking rates sharply brought prices down; an illustration of the liquidity channel, not evidence.
- [[dupraz-le-bihan-matheron-2022-make-up-strategies-with-finite-planning-horizons]]: A New Keynesian model with short-sighted households and firms but rational asset prices: forward guidance and make-up strategies (lower for longer, average inflation targeting, price-level targeting) still work through long rates, but less than textbook models say.
- [[durdu-martin-zer-2019-us-monetary-policy-in-global-banking-crises]]: A 1870–2010 panel of 69 countries: US monetary tightening raises the odds of a banking crisis abroad only for countries tied directly to the US through trade or dollar debt, through disorderly capital outflows, and mainly in emerging markets.
- [[fang-liu-roussanov-2022-getting-to-the-core-inflation-risks-across-asset-classes]]: Splits inflation into core and energy across eight asset classes, 1963–2019: "real" assets hedge energy inflation but not core, only core carries a (negative) risk premium, and the shift from core- to energy-driven inflation explains why the stock–bond correlation turned negative after 2000.
- [[keerati-2020-the-impact-of-monetary-policy-on-the-specialness-of-us-treasuries]]: An event study of Fed and ECB announcements, 2000–2019: a Fed tightening surprise that lifts the one-year yield 100bp raises the U.S. Treasury Premium over other G10 government bonds by 31–50bp, while ECB surprises barely move it.
- [[lenhard-2021-cartel-stability-in-times-of-low-interest-rates]]: A repeated-game model in which low interest rates both stabilise cartels (patience) and destabilise them (cheap capital to cheat), giving a U-shape in the rate, with suggestive support from 114 EU-convicted cartels.
- [[manzo-saret-2017-forecasting-inflation-like-a-data-scientist]]: A short Two Sigma commentary arguing for pooling many normalised inflation forecasts rather than trusting one measure, with a 2017 reading (median dip, negative skew); method only, no test.
- [[menand-2021-the-federal-reserve-and-the-2020-economic-and-financial-crisis]]: A legal map of the Fed's sixteen 2020 crisis facilities: six liquidity backstops for shadow banks and foreign central banks that lent over $1 trillion in a month, ten credit facilities that lent under $40 billion, and the statutes they stretched.

## Stock–bond correlation

- [[baele-van-holle-2017-stock-bond-correlations-macroeconomic-regimes-and-monetary-policy]]: Across 10 developed markets, inflation and output regimes alone barely explain the sign of the stock–bond correlation; it is strongly negative only when inflation is low and monetary policy accommodating, and positive whenever policy is restrictive or inflation high (panel R² 59%).
- [[bekaert-engstrom-2010-inflation-and-the-stock-market-the-fed-model]]: Why US dividend yields track nominal bond yields (correlation 0.78): not money illusion but stagflation, since high expected inflation has coincided with recessions, high risk aversion and high uncertainty that push equity premiums up; confirmed across 20 countries.
- [[brixton-thapar-maloney-2021-when-stock-bond-diversification-fails]]: AQR on inflation surprises, 1972–2021: stocks and bonds both lose, so their correlation turns positive when inflation news dominates; commodities, breakevens, trend and macro momentum offset it (hypothetical backfilled basket).
- [[burkhardt-hasseltoft-2012-understanding-asset-correlations]]: Stock–bond correlations move inversely with the correlation of growth and inflation: a regime-switching model finds countercyclical inflation (positive stock–bond correlation) until a 2001 break to procyclical inflation (negative), inflation uncertainty raising or lowering bond prices by regime, and the same mechanism explaining the Fed model.
- [[calderini-skilton-2021-macro-economic-drivers-of-the-bond-stock-correlation]]: A manager's four-factor model (growth, monetary policy, hedging premium, common premium) of the US stock–bond correlation: bonds hedge growth scares but not inflation or tightening scares, which is why the correlation flipped around 2000; latent factors with no standard errors.
- [[campbell-pflueger-viceira-2020-macroeconomic-drivers-of-bond-and-equity-risks]]: Why Treasuries switched from risky to hedging around 2001: the correlation between inflation and the output gap turned from negative to positive, and time-varying risk premia from habit preferences amplify the resulting flip in the bond–stock correlation (model −0.66 vs data −0.64 after the break).
- [[czasonis-kritzman-turkington-2021-the-stock-bond-correlation]]: Forecasting the five-year stock–bond correlation instead of extrapolating monthly returns: a single-period correlation measure, four macro and valuation predictors, and relevance-filtered (partial sample) regression; forecast–outcome correlation rises from 0.23 to 0.63, though the best model is not significant on the authors' own shuffling test.
- [[demirovic-guermat-tucker-2017-the-relationship-between-equity-and-bond-returns]]: The firm-level stock–bond correlation (a company's shares against its own bonds), 1996–2011: small and positive on average, rising sharply as distance to default falls, lowered by equity volatility as Merton's model predicts, and flattening out when the VIX is extreme.
- [[durham-2019-us-treasury-bond-betas-1961-2019]]: Daily Treasury–S&P 500 co-movement 1961–2019 through asymmetric DCC, spectral and quantile regressions: the correlation turned negative after 1998 but became more variable, short-cycle hedging is no better than long-cycle, and long Treasuries are not a convex hedge on the worst stock days.
- [[li-et-al-2022-does-fiscal-policy-matter-for-stock-bond-return-correlation]]: A New Keynesian model in which the US stock–bond correlation flipped from positive (1971–2001) to negative because policy switched from monetary-led to fiscal-led, changing which shocks drive both markets while bonds kept a positive premium.
- [[liu-fan-2016-stock-bond-correlation-and-duration-risk-allocation]]: Weekly stock–bond correlations from 10-minute futures data forecast Treasury returns: after unusually negative correlations the 10-year bond outperforms for about eight weeks (Sharpe 0.66, 1997–2013, about 3.4% a year alpha after factor controls); in-sample, pre-cost.
- [[shen-weisberger-2021-us-stock-bond-correlation-macroeconomic-drivers]]: A manager's map from macro policy to the sign of the US stock–bond correlation (−0.16, +0.28, −0.29 across 1950–65, 1965–2000, 2000–20): rate volatility, the growth–rates correlation and co-moving risk premia, with fiscal and monetary regimes behind them and advice to plan on zero; argument and descriptive statistics, not an estimated model.
- [[soper-2015-vix-and-us-treasuries-during-quantitative-easing]]: A short regression study, 2005–2015, finding that 10-year Treasury yields tend to fall in months when the VIX rises (flight to quality), with no measurable change during QE1–QE3; the paper calls it prediction, but most of its models are same-month co-movement.
- [[zeng-zhao-2022-expectation-driven-term-structure-of-equity-and-bond-yields]]: An equilibrium model with extrapolative, survey-fitted growth expectations and a constant risk premium that explains the equity yield curve, predictable dividend-strip returns and the post-2000 negative stock–bond correlation (mostly via tighter real GDP–dividend expectations, partly via procyclical inflation).

## Safe-haven assets

- [[barnes-et-al-2009-a-tips-scorecard]]: A Boston Fed review of whether TIPS work as an inflation hedge: CPI bias and the choice of price index add little systematic basis risk, TIPS held to maturity lock in a real yield, but held for a year they are mostly real-rate risk, and breakevens are not inflation forecasts.
- [[baur-dimpfl-2022-a-safe-haven-index]]: An equal-weighted index of seven safe havens (gold, Swiss franc, yen, US and German government bonds), used as a benchmark: havens are volatile, their volatility rises in crises, diversifying among them works, and Bitcoin and big tech stocks do not behave like them.
- [[baur-mcdermott-2013-financial-turmoil-and-safe-haven-assets]]: Treasuries and gold as safe havens from world stock crashes, 1980–2010: bonds rally the same day, gold the day after and more strongly in the most extreme falls, with an ambiguity-aversion story for why investors want gold.
- [[conlon-mcgee-2020-safe-haven-or-risky-hazard-bitcoin-during-the-covid-19-bear-market]]: Bitcoin in the COVID-19 crash: it fell with the S&P 500 and by more, and any allocation raised the portfolio's modified VaR and CVaR, so it was no safe haven in its first real equity crash (one five-week episode).
- [[garcia-feijoo-jensen-johnson-2012-the-effectiveness-of-asset-classes-in-hedging-risk]]: Which assets protect US stock and bond portfolios, 1970–2010: bonds co-crash with equities in only 5% of the worst equity months and gold in about 10%, energy tends to compound equity crashes, and REITs and foreign equities co-crash over 40% of the time; commodities hedge best when Fed policy is inflationary.
- [[ji-zhang-zhao-2020-searching-for-safe-haven-assets-during-covid-19]]: Tail-based tests of gold, bitcoin, EUR/USD, CNY/USD, crude and soybeans as safe havens for US, European and Chinese equities in the first COVID crash: only gold and soybean futures kept stable left tails and fixed the equity tail in a two-asset portfolio, on about 87 crisis-period days.
- [[kaczmarek-et-al-2022-false-safe-haven-assets]]: A volatility-targeting S&P 500 strategy that moves part of its cash into one of 13 candidate safe havens when forecast volatility is high: over 2010–2020 only long-term US Treasuries help, gold does not, and ARFIMA forecasts work as well as the neural networks.
- [[page-panariello-2018-when-diversification-fails]]: Left-tail versus right-tail correlations with US stocks across international equities, credit, alternatives, hedge-fund styles and risk factors (1970–2017): diversification across risk assets largely disappears in the worst 1% of months, and only Treasuries and momentum-type factors decouple.
- [[rubbaniy-et-al-2022-safe-haven-properties-of-soft-commodities-during-covid-19]]: Wavelet coherence between six soft commodities and a COVID-19 fear index in 2020: staples (wheat, corn, cotton, cocoa) moved with the fear index early on and coffee and sugar did not, though no stock data are used, so the safe-haven claim is untested.

## Global macro and asset allocation

- [[aqr-2022-capital-market-assumptions-for-major-asset-classes]]: AQR's yield-based 5–10 year expected real returns at end-2021 (US 60/40 about 2% real, half its historical average), with a look back at why its 2012 forecast of 2.4% turned into 8.5% realised, and suggestions to raise expected returns.
- [[asness-israelov-liew-2011-international-diversification-works-eventually]]: Markets crash together, so global diversification barely helps in the worst month, but over 5–15 years country-specific economic performance dominates and a global portfolio cuts the worst real losses sharply (22 countries, 1950–2008).
- [[azqueta-gavaldon-et-al-2020-nowcasting-business-cycle-turning-points]]: US recessions nowcast from Granger-causality networks of S&P 500 stock returns and machine-learning classifiers: near-perfect in sample, modest out of sample (AUROC 0.62–0.76 over 2007–2019).
- [[bakrania-et-al-2019-fundamental-trends-and-dislocated-markets]]: AQR's case for combining macro momentum (underreaction to economic news) with deep-value dislocation trades (overreaction) in one global macro strategy; a hypothetical 1993–2018 backtest with low correlation to stocks, bonds and macro hedge funds.
- [[bianchi-buchner-tamoni-2021-bond-risk-premia-with-machine-learning]]: Trees and neural networks forecast one-year US Treasury excess returns far better out of sample than principal components or penalised regressions (1990–2018), with the best results from a network built around economic groups of macro variables; the forecasts track risk aversion and growth uncertainty.
- [[boudoukh-et-al-2019-optimal-currency-hedging-for-international-equity-portfolios]]: Splits the optimal currency position of an international equity portfolio into a variance-minimising hedge (best estimated with a full covariance model) and a currency-style alpha portfolio (best built from simple carry, momentum and value ranks); G-10, 1981–2017, Sharpe 0.38 to 0.68 at lower volatility.
- [[brooks-2017-a-half-century-of-macro-momentum]]: AQR's systematic global macro strategy, going long assets whose growth, inflation, trade, policy and sentiment trends are improving, backtested from 1970: positive in every decade, strong in equity bear markets and rising real yields, and diversifying against price trend following.
- [[brunnermeier-nagel-pedersen-2008-carry-trades-and-currency-crashes]]: Why the currency carry trade carries crash risk: high-rate currencies are negatively skewed, crashes come when speculators hit funding constraints and unwind (VIX and TED spikes), and controlling for the VIX weakens the forward-premium puzzle.
- [[chernenko-sunderam-2020-measuring-the-perceived-liquidity-of-the-corporate-bond-market]]: A holdings-based measure of how liquid fund managers think bonds are (the slope of mutual-fund cash buffers on flow volatility): junk, unrated and Rule 144A bonds look much less liquid, and their perceived liquidity never recovered after 2008 even though trade-based measures say it did.
- [[diamonte-liew-stevens-1996-political-risk-in-emerging-and-developed-markets]]: Changes in analysts' political-risk ratings line up with emerging-market returns (11% a quarter between upgraded and downgraded countries, 1985–1995) far more than with developed-market returns; contemporaneous, so association rather than forecast.
- [[graham-harvey-2016-the-equity-risk-premium-in-2016]]: The 2016 edition of the Duke/CFO survey: CFOs' expected 10-year S&P 500 premium over Treasuries was 4.02% (3.58% average since 2000), barely moving over time while correlating with the VIX and credit spreads, and far below the WACCs and hurdle rates CFOs actually use.
- [[graham-harvey-2018-the-equity-risk-premium-in-2018]]: The Duke CFO survey's forward-looking equity risk premium, 2000–2017: about 3.6% on average and 4.4% at end-2017, barely moving through recessions, with CFO-reported WACCs and hurdle rates far above what the premium implies.
- [[huang-et-al-2018-sentiment-across-asset-markets]]: News- and social-media sentiment (Thomson Reuters MarketPsych) for US stocks, bonds, commodities, the dollar and housing, 1998–2016: each market's sentiment predicts its own next-month return, sentiment spills across markets, and combining all five raises predictability; small R² outside commodities.
- [[kay-et-al-2012-building-a-better-commodities-portfolio]]: AQR's case for holding commodities risk-balanced across sectors, at a constant 18% volatility, with drawdown control and tactical tilts on carry, trend and fundamentals, instead of through energy-heavy passive indices like the GSCI.
- [[kinlaw-kritzman-turkington-2021-a-new-index-of-the-business-cycle]]: A recession index built from Mahalanobis distances to past recessions and past booms in four series (industrial production, payrolls, stock returns, yield slope), with variable importance that shifts with conditions; it rose before every recession since 1956.
- [[liu-moench-2016-what-predicts-us-recessions]]: Probit models scored by ROC area pit about 35 leading indicators against the Treasury term spread for predicting US recessions 3–24 months ahead: the spread is best near one year, stock returns and payrolls add most at short horizons, margin debt and capital-goods orders at long ones.
- [[merton-1974-on-the-pricing-of-corporate-debt]]: Merton's structural credit model: equity is a call option on the firm's assets and debt is riskless debt minus a put, so credit spreads depend only on leverage and asset volatility.
- [[moreno-badia-et-al-2020-debt-is-not-free]]: An IMF random-forest early-warning study of fiscal crises in 188 countries (1980–2016): public, especially external, debt is the strongest and highly non-linear predictor (sharp rise near 70% of GDP in advanced economies), while the interest–growth differential gives almost no warning.
- [[stockl-kaiser-2020-higher-moments-matter]]: Cross-sectional dispersion, skewness and kurtosis of monthly returns as equity-premium predictors, 1963–2015: higher moments add out-of-sample power at 1–48 month horizons, but only after 2000 and across many tested combinations.
