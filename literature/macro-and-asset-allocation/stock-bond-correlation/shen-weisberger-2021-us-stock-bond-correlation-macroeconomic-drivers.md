## What it is

A practitioner's map from macro policy to the sign of the stock–bond correlation, written
when it had been negative for twenty years and inflation was starting to rise.

- **Three regimes.** Using a centred five-year rolling correlation of monthly returns:
  1950–1965 averages −0.16, 1965–2000 +0.28, 2000–2020 −0.29.
- **Three components.** Writing both prices as discounted cash flows, the stock–bond
  covariance is approximately the sum of (i) the volatility of the risk-free rate, which
  pushes the correlation up; (ii) the covariance of equity cash flows (growth) with rates,
  which pushes it down when rates rise with growth; and (iii) the covariance of the equity
  and bond risk premia.
- **What the data show.** 3-month yields averaged 6.4% in the positive regime against 2.3%
  and 1.6% in the negative ones, with two to three times the volatility. The growth–rates
  correlation was −0.33 in 1965–2000 and +0.2 in 2000–2020, as the framework predicts, but
  also negative in 1950–1965 when the stock–bond correlation was negative, which it does
  not. CPI inflation was 4.3% a year in the positive regime against 1.3% and 1.8%.
- **Policy mapping.** Sustainable fiscal policy, independent and rules-based monetary policy
  and demand-driven cycles support a negative correlation. Unsustainable fiscal policy,
  discretionary or fiscally coordinated monetary policy and supply shocks support a
  positive one. Deviations of the fed funds rate from a Taylor rule are used to date
  discretionary periods.
- **Portfolio arithmetic.** With stocks at 10% return and 12% volatility and bonds at 5%
  and 6%, a 60/40 portfolio has about 7% volatility at a correlation of −0.3 and 7.5% at
  zero. Holding 7% volatility then needs about 8 points less in stocks (roughly 0.4% a year
  less return); at +0.3 the stock weight falls to about 40%, costing another 0.5%.
- **Recommendation.** Investors without a strong macro view should plan on a zero
  correlation. Three scenarios are sketched, two of which (shifting Fed priorities with
  expansionary fiscal policy; fiscal-sustainability worries with an accommodative Fed) end
  in a positive correlation.

## Why it's in the library

- It is the practitioner's version of the stock–bond regime story, with the components
  made explicit enough to watch: rate volatility, the growth–rates correlation and
  co-movement of risk premia.
- Written in May 2021, before the 2022 episode in which stocks and bonds fell together; the
  "assume zero" advice reads well in hindsight. That is a dated call, not a tested model.
- It is argument and descriptive statistics, not an estimated model: the decomposition is
  approximate, the regime boundaries are drawn by eye, and the policy mapping comes from
  three episodes. Its own 1950–1965 numbers do not fit its growth–rates channel.
