## What it is

A long, sceptical audit of the six stories told for owning gold, written when gold was near
its 2011–2012 peak. Gold had risen more than 15.4% a year from December 1999 to March 2012,
against 2.5% inflation, 1.5% a year for US stocks and 6.4% for bonds.

- **"Gold is an inflation hedge."** Over centuries, maybe: a Roman centurion under Augustus
  earned about 38.6 ounces of gold a year, about 30% more than a modern US Army captain's
  pay in gold. Over practical horizons, no. Gold does not track unexpected inflation: the
  annual relation is flat apart from one year, 1980. Trailing ten-year real gold returns
  ranged from about −6% to +20% a year while ten-year inflation only ranged from 2.3% to
  7.3%.
- **Valuation.** The real price of gold (gold divided by the CPI) is treated like a
  valuation ratio. Since 1975 a high real price has been followed by low ten-year real
  returns. Read literally, the 2012 level implied about −10% a year in real terms over the
  next decade. The authors call this a "known unknown", not a stable law.
- **"Gold is a currency hedge."** Regressing seven currencies' monthly moves against the
  dollar on gold's monthly return gives betas averaging only about −0.15, with low R². Real
  gold prices have moved together across countries far more than exchange rates moved.
- **"Gold does well when real yields are low."** The correlation between the 10-year TIPS
  real yield and the real gold price since 1997 is −0.82. But both series trend (correlations
  with a time trend of about −0.90 and +0.87), so the relation may be spurious, and in UK
  data it falls to −0.31.
- **"Gold is a safe haven."** In 17% of months from 1975 to 2012, stocks and gold both fell.
  Gold is a good diversifier (low correlation with stocks) but not a reliable haven. Through
  Brazil's hyperinflation from 1980 to 2000 gold lost about 70% of its real purchasing
  power, about the same as for a US investor over those years.
- **"Gold is money" and "gold is underowned".** Supply has barely responded to price: mine
  output stayed around 2,500 tonnes a year while the price rose fivefold, and the USGS
  estimated about 20 years of minable reserves. Investment demand rose with price (a price
  elasticity of about +0.98, i.e. momentum buying), while jewellery demand fell
  (about −0.24). Investors own only about 20% of the roughly $9 trillion of gold above
  ground. If emerging-market central banks held as much gold per head or per unit of GDP as
  developed ones, real prices could rise much further.
- **The dilemma.** Either the real price mean-reverts (the "golden constant") or emerging
  market demand is a structural break ("this time is different"). The paper does not choose.

## Why it's in the library

**It takes apart the standard arguments one by one, with data.** Each popular reason to own
gold gets its own test, and most fail at investment horizons. It is the reference for why
"gold hedges inflation" should be heard as "over a century, perhaps".

**It frames gold as a bet on its own real price.** Gold has no cash flows, so its expected
return comes down to where the real price goes from here. That makes the real-price ratio
the natural, if noisy, valuation measure, and it puts mean reversion against structural
demand change as the central question.

**It flags a widely used correlation as possibly spurious.** The TIPS–gold relation is often
quoted as a pricing model for gold. The paper shows how much of it comes from two trending
series over a short sample.

**Read the forecast with hindsight.** The paper's mean-reversion reading pointed to falling
real gold prices. Gold did fall from 2012 to 2015 but then rose to new highs above $2,000 an
ounce by 2020, so its own "this time is different" branch got its turn. The authors were
explicit that they could not tell which branch would win.
