## What it is

A study of whether gold-mining shares behaved like gold during the COVID-19 crash. They did
not.

- **The sample.** 230 ASX-listed gold companies, split by business stage: 33 producers, 25
  project developers and 171 explorers. Daily returns are regressed on the ASX market and
  on the gold price (in US$ and A$). Dummies and interaction terms let the market and gold
  exposures differ by firm type and between normal times and the COVID crash of early 2020.
- **Normal times.** Exposures follow the business model. Producers have the highest gold
  beta (0.76 on average) and the lowest market beta (0.18); explorers the reverse (gold
  0.24, market 0.27); developers sit between (0.36 and 0.37). Idiosyncratic risk dominates:
  even producers have an average R² of only 11%. Since 2017, producers had beaten
  developers, who beat explorers, but all lagged gold; explorers fell more than 50% while
  gold rose.
- **The crash.** All three types fell together and by similar amounts, in line with the
  ASX 100, even though gold rose. Explorers were hit hardest, at about −1% a day of
  abnormal return. Market exposure rose for every type, more so with gold in A$. The
  reason the market won is size: the market fell more than 20% while gold rose only a few
  percent, so similar betas meant a large net loss.
- **Gold itself.** Gold dipped with stocks at the start of the crisis before recovering.
  The authors suggest this was investors selling gold to cover losses elsewhere, which is
  still gold doing its insurance job. Because the Australian dollar fell, gold in A$ hit
  records, and miners decoupled from it even further.

## Why it's in the library

**A gold equity is an equity first in a crash.** The safe-haven property belongs to bullion,
not to companies that mine it. In the COVID shock, diversification across gold firms of very
different risk did nothing, because all equities fell together.

**It is a clean case of contagion.** Firms' fundamentals improved (higher gold price)
while their shares fell with the market. That separates a sentiment-driven fall from a
fundamental one, which is usually impossible when both move the same way.

**The implied mispricing is an opinion.** The authors argue miners became undervalued
relative to gold and should "recouple". They do not test whether that happened.
