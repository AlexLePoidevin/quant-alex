## What it is

A short theory paper explaining why gold and land rose much faster than consumer prices
in the inflationary 1970s. The answer is the tax system, not a flight from money.

- **The puzzle.** Textbook theory says a change in the inflation rate should not change
  relative prices in the long run: every price, including land and gold, should simply
  inflate at the new rate. But from 1968 to 1977 US consumer prices rose 74% while gold
  went from $39 to more than $147 an ounce (+250%), farmland rose about 150% and housing
  about 110%.
- **The mechanism.** Taxes are not indexed to inflation. Interest is taxed in full at the
  income tax rate, including the part that only compensates for inflation. A nominal gain
  on land or gold is taxed only as a capital gain, at a lower rate and only when sold. So
  when expected inflation rises and nominal interest rates rise one-for-one with it, the
  after-tax real yield on bonds falls by more than the after-tax real yield on land or
  gold. Investors switch, and the relative price of the real asset jumps until yields are
  equal again.
- **The condition.** The relative price of land (or gold) rises with inflation whenever
  dr/dπ < (1 − c)/(1 − θ), where r is the nominal interest rate, π inflation, θ the income
  tax rate and c the effective capital gains rate. With dr/dπ ≈ 1, as US history suggested,
  this holds whenever c < θ, which is true for every taxable investor. With no taxes
  (θ = c = 0) inflation has no effect, which is the textbook result.
- **Gold.** Gold pays no yield, so its after-tax real return is just −cπ. A portfolio
  model in which demand for gold relative to bonds rises with the yield gap gives the same
  condition: higher expected inflation raises the relative price of gold once, and after
  that it inflates at the general rate.
- **The size of it.** With θ = 0.4 and c = 0.15, the pre-tax real yield on land falls by
  0.4 times any rise in inflation. Starting from an 8% real yield, 6% inflation cuts it by
  30%, which raises the relative price of land by 43%; 10% inflation halves the yield and
  doubles the relative price. Feldstein says these numbers are crude and probably overstate
  the effect, and that gold, with no real yield at all, is likely to be more sensitive than
  land.

## Why it's in the library

**It is the tax explanation for "gold is an inflation hedge".** The usual story is that gold
holds its purchasing power. Feldstein's mechanism is different: gold's relative price jumps
when expected inflation rises because unindexed taxes punish bonds more than gold. That
predicts a one-off repricing when the inflation outlook shifts, not a steady one-for-one
tracking of the price level, which fits the later evidence that gold tracks inflation badly
over practical horizons.

**It ties gold to real yields through a concrete channel.** The after-tax real yield on
bonds is what gold competes against. That is the same comparison behind the observed
negative correlation between TIPS real yields and the real price of gold, which Erb and
Harvey later question as possibly spurious.

**It is theory with one decade of motivating data.** There is no estimation, the economy is
closed and stationary, and the paper itself notes that inflation uncertainty and speculation
were left out. Tax rules have also changed a lot since 1978. Treat it as a mechanism to keep
in mind, not as a measured effect.
