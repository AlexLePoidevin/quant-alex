## What it is

Evidence that US monetary easing made the dollar *stronger* during the 2008–2012 crisis,
the opposite of textbook theory, and an explanation.

- **Identification.** Policy surprises are changes in interest-rate futures in a one-hour
  window around FOMC statements and QE announcements, used as instruments (2SLS). Outcomes
  are quarterly changes in nine G10 exchange rates against the dollar, 1991–2015, with the
  crisis period defined as 2008:Q4–2012:Q2.
- **The main result.** During the crisis, a surprise that lowered US medium- and long-term
  forward rates relative to foreign ones made the dollar appreciate, significantly so for
  horizons of 12 quarters or more. Before and after the crisis the response has the
  conventional sign. First-stage F-statistics exceed 39.
- **Not every currency.** The appreciation was strongest against "non-hedge" currencies,
  those that weaken when US growth falls (AUD, CAD, NOK, NZD, SEK, GBP). Against the
  "hedge" currencies (CHF, JPY, and weakly EUR) the dollar fell or barely moved.
- **Two channels.** A decomposition, with expectations disciplined by survey forecasts in
  a VAR, finds two causes. First, easing lowered the excess return investors demanded for
  holding US bonds, which is a flight to safety. Second, it lowered expected US inflation
  relative to other countries.
- **Why: the information effect.** The Fed's calendar-based forward guidance ("low rates
  until date X") came with statements that the economy would stay weak. Markets read it as
  bad news. A 1-point fall in a US forward rate cut survey GDP-growth expectations by 0.7–1
  point and raised a measure of aggregate risk aversion by 28–43%.
- **Wealth transfer.** The same easing reduced the US net foreign asset position by up to
  18% of US GDP, 17% through valuation effects. That matches the size of the "exorbitant
  duty": when the dollar rises in bad times, the US effectively pays the rest of the world
  insurance.
- **A model.** A stylised model, in which the central bank knows more than the market,
  reproduces all of this. It applies when uncertainty about the economy is high relative
  to uncertainty about policy, as it was in 2008–2012.

## Why it's in the library

**It explains why the dollar can rally in a crisis even as the Fed eases.** For
cross-asset hedging this matters: the dollar's safe-haven behaviour against commodity
currencies held even against policy that "should" have weakened it, while yen and franc
behaved differently.

**It is a careful example of the "information effect" of monetary policy.** Markets can
read an easing as bad news about the economy, which shows up in growth expectations and
risk aversion.

**The magnitudes come from a short, unusual period.** About 15 quarters, with the euro
crisis included. The authors confirm a structural break, but the crisis result is not a
general rule for how the dollar responds to Fed policy.
