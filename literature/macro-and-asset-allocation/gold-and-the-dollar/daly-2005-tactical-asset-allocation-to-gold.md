## What it is

A proposal to time gold with the "Fed model", the comparison of the S&P 500's forward
earnings yield with the 10-year Treasury yield.

- **Gold on its own.** From January 1968 to July 2004, gold returned 2.67% a year over cash
  with 21.4% volatility and a market correlation of about zero. From 1979 to 2004 it
  returned −2.4% a year. Adding a static 10% or 20% of gold to the S&P 500 over the last 10,
  20 or 30 years lowered volatility but did not raise returns.
- **The signal.** The Fed model's "fair value" for the S&P 500 is the price at which its
  earnings yield equals the 10-year yield. The author reads a market well below fair value
  as a time of inflation fear, when money leaves stocks and bonds for gold. The rule: hold
  gold from the month after the market is 15% or more undervalued until it no longer is.
- **The claimed results.** Holding gold after any undervalued reading earned 8.48% a year
  over cash; after a 15%-plus reading, about 27% from 1968 and over 39% from 1979. Adding
  20–40% gold to a Dow portfolio on the 15% signal is said to raise returns and lower
  volatility, with the signal catching the 1980 gold peak.
- **The Dow/gold ratio.** A closing section argues, citing Mises and Greenspan's 1966
  essay, that credit booms end with the Dow/gold ratio at 1 or 2, and suggests "10,000 Dow
  and 10,000 gold".

## Why it's in the library

**Mainly as a cautionary example.** The paper shows the pattern that data-snooping tests
are designed to catch.
- *The threshold was picked after looking.* The 15% threshold is chosen because it worked
  best; 10% and 20% are mentioned as "less significant".
- *No actual test is reported.* The abstract claims statistical significance, but the
  paper shows no test statistics.
- *The pre-1978 earnings carry look-ahead.* Before 1978 there were no analyst forecasts,
  so the paper uses next year's *realised* earnings as the "forecast", which the investor
  could not have known.
- *Very few independent signals.* The strongest results rest on a handful of
  1970s–1980 episodes.

**The economic idea is still worth knowing.** The idea is that high expected inflation
cheapens stocks relative to bonds (Modigliani–Cohn inflation illusion) and favours real
assets. The Fed model itself is widely criticised for comparing a real yield with a
nominal one, which the author acknowledges.
