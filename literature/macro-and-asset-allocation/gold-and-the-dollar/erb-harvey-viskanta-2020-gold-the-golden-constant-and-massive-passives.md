## What it is

A short warning, written in mid-2020, that gold looked expensive as an inflation hedge. It
also offers a reason the price might rise anyway.

- **The golden-constant decomposition.** The nominal gold price is the product of an
  inflation index (the CPI) and the *real* gold price (gold ÷ CPI), just as a stock price is
  earnings times a P/E. It is an accounting identity, not a model. Over rolling five-year
  windows since 1975, almost all the variation in gold's return comes from the real price,
  not from inflation. Gold's best five-year annualised return was about 37% and its worst
  about −15%, while five-year inflation only ranged between 1.2% and 10%.
- **Where the real price stood.** In mid-2020 the real gold price (about 7.8 on their scale)
  was nearly as high as at the January 1980 and August 2011 peaks. In the five years after
  those peaks the nominal price fell 55% and 28%, and the real price 67% and 33%. Both peaks
  came at times of fear about future inflation: stagflation in 1980, quantitative easing in
  2011.
- **"Gold is a bond" versus "massive passives".** The popular explanation is that gold moves
  inversely with real yields on inflation-protected Treasuries. The authors test both
  stories for a long-run equilibrium (cointegration). Real yields and the real gold price
  show none, so they call that relationship as spurious as the one between Equatorial
  Guinea's population and the S&P 500 (Exhibit 8). The gold held by the two largest gold
  ETFs and the real gold price do appear cointegrated.
- **The ETF story.** "Massive passives" is a regulator's term for large, price-insensitive
  index buyers. In sample, each doubling of ETF gold holdings went with a doubling of the
  real price. The authors note that extrapolating this is a stretch. They point to the
  Hunt brothers' silver corner as an example of too much money chasing too little metal.
- **Scenario grid.** The identity turns beliefs into prices. A constant real price of 7.8
  and 1.56% inflation (the 10-year breakeven in July 2020) gives $2,328 in ten years. A
  10.56% inflation rate with the real price rising to 38.9 gives $27,289. The framework
  says nothing about which belief is right.

## Why it's in the library

**It separates the inflation-hedge story from what actually drives the gold price.** Over
horizons investors care about, the real price of gold moves far more than inflation does,
so "gold hedges inflation" is not something the available data can confirm. The authors'
point is that the data are too short to tell either way.

**It shows a favourite chart failing a basic test.** Gold against real yields looks
compelling, but the two series are not cointegrated. This is a good example of a
relationship that is plotted far more often than it is tested.

**It is valuation reasoning applied to an asset with no cash flows.** A high real price has
historically been followed by low real returns. The paper is careful that this is
tendency, not timing: a high valuation does not say when the fall comes. The ETF-flow
story could carry the price higher first, as with stocks between 1996 and 2000.

**Read it as argument, not evidence.** The cointegration results are stated, not tabled,
and two peaks are a very small sample.
