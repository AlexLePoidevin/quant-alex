## What it is

An update of *The Golden Dilemma* that turns the "golden constant" idea (gold keeps its
purchasing power, so its long-run real return is zero) into a fair-value number and a
ten-year return forecast.

- **The fair value.** Divide the gold price by the US CPI. From 1975 the ratio averaged
  about 3.46. Multiply the average ratio by today's CPI (239.9 in June 2016) and you get a
  "golden constant" value of about $840 an ounce, against a market price of $1,321.
- **The range.** The ratio has been as high as 8.73 and as low as 1.47; in mid-2016 it was
  about 5.50. If it revisited its old low, gold would be worth about $353 an ounce.
- **Valuation and returns.** Since 1975, an above-average real price of gold has been
  followed by below-average ten-year real gold returns, as with valuation ratios for stocks.
- **The forecast.** Assume 2% inflation for ten years. If gold reverts to fair value
  (then $1,024), the real return is about −4.4% a year; if it overshoots to its old low
  (then about $430), about −12.4% a year. The real return is the same whatever inflation
  you assume, because the scenarios fix the end real price.
- **Knowing inflation does not help.** Rolling ten-year US inflation varied little while
  gold returns swung widely. Even a perfect inflation forecast would barely have improved a
  forecast of ten-year nominal gold returns. Knowing the future real price is what
  matters. In the authors' words, ten years is not the long run for gold.

## Why it's in the library

**It shows what a valuation model for gold looks like, and how weak it is.** Gold has no
cash flows, so "fair value" can only come from an assumption, here a constant real price.
The paper is explicit that this is one hypothesis among several (market efficiency, Chinese
demand, mining costs), not a fact.

**The forecast failed, which is the useful lesson.** Ten years from the paper's June 2016
starting point, gold had not fallen towards $840 or $430: it traded above $2,000 an ounce by
2020. A long-horizon mean-reversion signal on a single asset, estimated from one 40-year
sample, can be wrong for a decade. The authors flag the data-mining risk in long-horizon
predictability charts themselves.

**Small slips in the text.** The average ratio is given as 3.46 and then used as 3.49 in the
fair-value sum, and the overshoot price is quoted as both $350 and $353 an ounce, and with
both June 2015 and June 2016 CPI. None of this changes the argument.
