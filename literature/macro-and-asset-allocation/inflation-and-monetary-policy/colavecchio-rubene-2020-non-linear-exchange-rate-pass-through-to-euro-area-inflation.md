## What it is

An estimate of exchange rate pass-through (ERPT): how much of a move in the euro shows up
in import prices and consumer prices, how fast, and whether the answer depends on the sign
or the size of the move.

- **Method.** Single-equation local projections of the change in import prices (at the
  border) and HICP consumer prices at horizons up to eight quarters, on the nominal
  effective exchange rate with controls for foreign costs and domestic slack. Interaction
  terms split depreciations from appreciations, and "large" moves (above one standard
  deviation of the quarterly change; 29% of the 87 euro area observations) from small
  ones.
- **Linear pass-through, euro area.** Import prices absorb 20% of an exchange rate change on
  impact and 33% after a year; after two years the estimate is no longer significant.
  Consumer prices: about 2% on impact and 4% after one and two years, none significant.
- **Size matters, sign does not.** For large moves, import-price pass-through is 23% on
  impact and 49% after a year; small moves show no significant pass-through beyond impact.
  Consumer prices respond significantly only to large moves (3% on impact, 8% after a
  year). Appreciations and depreciations are not statistically different.
- **Countries differ.** In the original member states import-price pass-through is 20–40% on
  impact and 30–60% after a year (Italy highest at 63%); complete pass-through is rejected
  everywhere. Pass-through to consumer prices is small in every country.

## Why it's in the library

- It gives orders of magnitude for one link in the macro chain behind inflation regimes: a
  large currency move feeds into euro area import prices within a year, but only a few
  percent reaches consumer prices.
- The size non-linearity matters for scenario thinking: small currency moves can be
  ignored, large ones cannot.
- It is a macro paper with no asset-pricing content and only an indirect link to markets;
  its place is as background to the inflation material in this topic. Wide confidence
  bands beyond one year, as the authors note. One inconsistency: the abstract says consumer
  prices "respond significantly after one year", but in the linear model (Table 1) they do
  not; they do only for large moves.
