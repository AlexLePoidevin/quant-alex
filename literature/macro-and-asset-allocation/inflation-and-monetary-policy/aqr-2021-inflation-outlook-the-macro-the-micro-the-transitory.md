## What it is

A short commentary on the 2021 US inflation surge. Headline CPI rose from 1.7% year on
year in February 2021 to 5.4% in June; core CPI from 1.3% to 4.5%, its highest in three
decades. Markets had reacted calmly, which AQR reads as investors accepting that the
surge was "transitory".

The note splits CPI into custom buckets:

- **Reopening sectors** (restaurants, air travel, face-to-face services), autos and
  energy: these drove most of the spike. New and used vehicles are about 7% of CPI but
  accounted for over 40% of the rise in the three months to June, with used car prices
  up 45% in a year. The 3-month annualised CPI rate fell from close to 10% in June to below
  5% by September, which supports the transitory view.
- **Everything else** (CPI excluding autos, energy and reopening items): this kept
  accelerating, to about a 5% annualised pace, which AQR takes as evidence of a broader
  upturn.

The macro case against "transitory": a fiscal deficit of over $5 trillion since early
2020, largely matched by Fed asset purchases and by a similar rise in bank deposits (the
note compares this to Friedman's "helicopter money"); consumer spending above its
pre-pandemic trend; a tight labour market, with private wage growth the fastest since
2007; and rents and owners' equivalent rent (about 30% of CPI) set to catch up with house
prices that were up 18.6% year on year. AQR concludes that CPI above 5% may be a
near-term peak, but a quick return to 2% looks unlikely, which favours commodities and
TIPS breakevens and calls for caution on stocks and bonds.

## Why it's in the library

It is a compact worked example of decomposing an inflation print into transitory and
persistent parts, and a dated forecast that can be checked against what happened next.
The note's view, that 2021 inflation was not purely transitory, proved right: US CPI went
on to peak above 9% in mid-2022.

It is commentary, not a study: there is no model or test, only charts of public data and
an argument. The asset-class implications rely on historical inflation sensitivities
measured elsewhere.
