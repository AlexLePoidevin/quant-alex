## What it is

A theory paper on who controls inflation when public debt is large: the central bank (the
price-level target holds, "monetary dominance"), the treasury (the price level jumps to
whatever keeps the government solvent, "fiscal dominance"), or bond investors.

- **The question.** Sargent and Wallace's "unpleasant monetarist arithmetic" (1981): if
  the treasury runs large enough deficits, the central bank must eventually print money to
  keep the state solvent, whatever its mandate. That result assumes the treasury can commit
  to its whole future deficit path and faces unlimited demand for its bonds.
- **What changes here.** Neither authority can commit, so who "moves first" is decided in
  equilibrium. And issuing more bonds or reserves raises the real interest rate, because it
  crowds out private investment.
- **Main result: markets can enforce monetary dominance.** To force the central bank to
  inflate, the treasury must spend its future fiscal capacity now, through a very large bond
  issue. That issue drives the real rate up. If the cost of borrowing so much at that rate
  exceeds the gain from inflating away legacy debt, the treasury does not try, and the
  central bank hits its target without commitment or fiscal backing. Otherwise the price
  level is set by solvency, as in the fiscal theory of the price level.
- **When each regime wins.** Monetary dominance is likelier with small legacy debt, a large
  crowding-out effect on rates, a large future tax capacity and a patient treasury.
- **Dynamics.** The regime can switch from monetary to fiscal dominance as the
  government's net wealth runs down. One equilibrium has years of low rates and on-target
  prices despite large deficits, followed by a pick-up in inflation and then fiscal
  consolidation: debt rises first, and inflation comes later.
- **"Market dominance."** If public debt can be a bubble, investors can enforce a price
  level by threatening to stop rolling it over.
- **Policy points.** Debt caps can stand in for market discipline but may not be credible
  when debt is high. A central bank needs assets whose value does not depend on the
  government's solvency. The quantity to watch is net public liabilities held by the
  private sector.

## Why it's in the library

- **A framework for the inflation regime.** Whether inflation is anchored by the central
  bank or by fiscal solvency decides how bonds behave: in fiscal dominance, nominal bonds
  stop hedging equities. This connects to the regime-based explanations of the
  stock–bond correlation elsewhere in the library.
- **A timing warning.** The model's equilibrium where inflation arrives only after a long
  period of low rates and large deficits is a reason not to read calm inflation data as
  proof that fiscal expansion is harmless.

It is pure theory with flexible prices and only short-term debt, and it is not calibrated
or tested on data.
