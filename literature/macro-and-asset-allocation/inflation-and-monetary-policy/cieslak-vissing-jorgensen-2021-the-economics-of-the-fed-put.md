## What it is

An empirical study of the "Fed put": the pattern that the Federal Reserve eases after the
stock market falls. It asks whether the pattern is real, why it exists, and whether the Fed
overreacts.

- **The pattern, from the mid-1990s on.** Excess stock returns between scheduled FOMC
  meetings predict the next policy moves. A 10% intermeeting fall predicts a 32bp lower
  federal funds target at the next meeting and 127bp lower after a year. Returns in the
  lowest quintile (about −8%) were followed by cuts averaging 119bp over the next eight
  meetings (1994–2008). The link is asymmetric: rises do not predict hikes. Before 1994
  the pattern is absent.
- **It runs through growth expectations.** Weak returns go with downgrades to the Fed
  staff's real-GDP forecasts: a 10% fall lowers expected growth by about 0.96 percentage
  points over two meetings. In a forward-looking Taylor rule, stock returns matter mainly
  through this channel and the Fed's nowcast, with a much weaker link to inflation.
- **The Fed watches the market.** There are 975 stock-market mentions in 184 FOMC minutes
  (1994–2016), about 14% as many as inflation mentions. Their tone tracks actual returns,
  and negative mentions predict cuts while positive mentions do not predict hikes.
- **Why it watches: mainly the wealth effect.** Classifying the mentions, 38% treat the
  market as a *driver* of the economy and 8% as a *predictor* of it. The dominant driver
  channel is the consumption wealth effect, at 22% of all mentions. The authors'
  illustration: in 2007, a 10% fall cuts household wealth by $1.78tn, which at a 4%
  wealth effect lowers GDP growth by about 0.5 point. In the chairs' own statements in the
  transcripts, the driver view outnumbers the predictor view about three to one (43%
  against 15%).
- **No ex post overreaction.** Compared with private forecasters and with how well stocks
  actually predict GDP and unemployment, the Fed does not update its growth forecasts too
  much on market moves.
- **Moral hazard is acknowledged but not acted on.** Of 97 dissents in 1994–2018, 76 were
  hawkish and many cite market risk-taking, but these concerns do not appear to change
  the decisions.

## Why it's in the library

- **The policy put is part of the return distribution.** After a sharp fall, the expected
  path of rates moves down. That cushions equity drawdowns and helps bonds rally in
  growth scares, and it is one mechanism behind the negative stock–bond correlation of
  the post-2000 period.
- **It explains an asymmetry.** The Fed reacts to falls and not to rises, which shortens
  some selloffs and leaves rallies alone, a background condition for any bear-market or
  "buy the dip" statistic since the mid-1990s.
- **The limits are clear.** The put works through growth expectations, so it is weaker when
  inflation is high and the Fed cannot cut. The sample ends in 2016 and does not include an
  inflationary regime.

Identification is by prediction and text, not a causal experiment. Market falls and Fed
cuts share common macro news, and the paper's case that the market itself matters rests on
the minutes and transcripts.
