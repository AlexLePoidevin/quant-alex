## What it is

A legal map of what the Fed did in 2020 and which laws it stretched to do it.

- **The scale.** In 2020 the Fed set up sixteen ad hoc lending facilities, lent over $1
  trillion to banks, financial firms, businesses, nonprofits and municipalities, and bought
  more than $2 trillion of financial assets.
- **Two kinds of facility.** Six were *liquidity* facilities (the PDCF for dealers, the MMLF
  for money funds, the CPFF, repo operations, the FIMA repo facility and foreign central
  bank swap lines). They stopped a run on "deposit substitutes" issued by shadow banks, and
  the author calls them a resounding success: over $1 trillion lent in under a month and the
  funding crisis quickly abated. Ten were *credit* facilities (corporate bond buying through
  the PMCCF and SMCCF, the Municipal Liquidity Facility, the Main Street programmes, TALF,
  the PPPLF and others). They extended credit to the real economy and lent less than $40
  billion over three quarters against stated capacities above $1.5 trillion. The Main
  Street programmes bought about 1,800 loans worth $16.5 billion out of $500 billion of
  capacity.
- **The legal problems.** Fifteen of the sixteen facilities conflict with one or more
  provisions of the Federal Reserve Act or the Gold Reserve Act. Up to seven credit
  facilities clash with the 2010 rule that emergency lending must be for "providing
  liquidity to the financial system"; Congress overrode it without saying so (*sub silentio*)
  through the CARES Act, which appropriated $500 billion for the Treasury to invest in Fed
  facilities. Repo operations with dealers and swap lines with foreign central banks, used
  for decades, are structured as purchases and sales "in the open market" but are in
  substance off-market loans that Congress has never expressly authorised.
- **The argument.** Using the Fed as a "national investment authority" mixes monetary and
  fiscal roles, reduces accountability, and tends to favour asset owners. The author
  proposes a separate public lending authority, a standing fiscal emergency fund, and
  regulating shadow banks as banks so that they can use the discount window under bank-like
  rules.

## Why it's in the library

**It explains the "Fed put" as an institutional fact.** In 2020 the Fed acted as dealer and
lender of last resort to the whole dollar system, including foreign central banks and money
funds, within weeks. That backstop is a large part of why the March 2020 crash reversed so
fast, and why markets now expect the same in the next crisis.

**It separates what worked from what did not.** Liquidity support to the financial system
was quick and decisive; credit support to the real economy was slow and small. The asymmetry
helps explain why asset prices recovered long before the economy did.

**It is a legal argument, not an empirical study.** The numbers are reported take-up and
announced capacities. The claims about distribution and accountability are the author's
reading of law and policy.
