## What it is

Two small classroom experiments on whether more money and lower interest rates push up
share prices, run with business students bidding for shares in a fictional company.

- **Game A, money supply (56 students).** Players held cash or bid for shares that paid no
  dividends. Ten new shares were auctioned each round, and the "central bank" doubled the
  money in players' hands each round up to round 5, then stopped. Average bids rose with
  the money supply, and when the expansion stopped, bids fell back, which the author calls
  a crash.
- **Game B, interest rates (43 students).** Players chose between shares and a fixed
  deposit. The base rate first fell, raising the company's profits and dividends through
  cheaper debt, then rose sharply in round 7. Share prices climbed as rates fell and
  collapsed after the hike, as players moved back into deposits.
- **Conclusion drawn.** Very loose policy (zero or negative rates, QE) encourages equity
  bubbles, and the exit should be slow to avoid a crash like 1929.

## Why it's in the library

- **A simple illustration of the liquidity and discount-rate channels.** It shows in a toy
  market how cash looking for yield bids up a fixed supply of shares, and how a sharp
  reversal in rates can turn that into a fall.

The evidence is weak. Each game was played once with one cohort, the experiments were cut
short by the pandemic, there are no statistical tests or controls, and in Game A the
shares pay nothing, so prices can only reflect cash on hand. The policy conclusions go well
beyond what the experiments show.
