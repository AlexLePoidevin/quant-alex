## Known for

Decomposes total stock volatility into **market**, **industry** and **firm-specific**
components without needing to estimate betas, and tracks each through time (1962–1997).

Two headline findings: firm-level idiosyncratic volatility **rose substantially** over the
period while market volatility did not; and consequently the **number of stocks required to
achieve a given level of diversification increased**.

## Why it's here

⭐ **The modern King (1966), and the quantitative backbone of the portfolio argument.** It
supplies the three-way decomposition *and* shows the shares are **not constants** — which is
the sloshing-tide idea measured rather than asserted.

The diversification result is the direct evidence for the piece's central inversion: the
idiosyncratic part of each stock is large but averages away, so a portfolio's risk converges
to the shared component even though each holding was mostly its own story.
